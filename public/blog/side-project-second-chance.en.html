When a side project goes to sleep at zero users and zero revenue — "let it rot" vs. "hand it off"

2026-07-27 · CoreLabs · For: developers who built an MVP/SaaS prototype but haven't touched it in a while

Why this, why now

The success stories still loom large in indie hacker and side-project communities, but the numbers underneath them are cold. Building has gotten easier (vibe coding, AI), growing something to the end has gotten harder, and more code sits asleep in between than ever.

  • Analyses of indie products verified through Stripe and similar sources keep circulating a figure: more than half (around 54%) sit at $0 revenue. Not "makes a little" — the majority never made a single dollar.
  • Posts like "I only got it after 8 failures" or "putting the indie hacking dream on hold" keep showing up as honest write-ups. Landing pages, AI wrappers, and weekend-shipped apps keep pouring out, and most drop out of anyone's attention within a month or two.
  • On the flip side, there are also cases of projects abandoned for years that got revived. The point isn't "every project has to die" — it's that if you don't have the energy to keep growing it, you need a different path.

1. Sound familiar? That folder isn't a failure — it's paused time

Does this sound familiar?

  • The repo's still alive on GitHub, and the README still says "Coming soon."
  • The landing page looks great, sign-ups haven't hit triple digits, and the Stripe dashboard is silent.
  • Every weekend you think "this time I'll market it," and then Monday shows up and your day job takes over.
  • The code, domain, server bills, and late nights are already spent — but there's no user feedback and no revenue.

This isn't just about being lazy. The indie scene itself is shifting toward a structure where shipping is easy but distribution, trust, and ongoing operation are hard. You don't see the failure stories mostly because people quietly let them sleep instead of posting them.

An abandoned side project isn't "trash" — it can be an asset that's still someone else's second chance. It's just that it may no longer be a priority for you, its current owner.

The moment you accept that gap, you get options. Keep holding on and stacking up guilt, or hand it off in a way that captures its value.

2. Why cashing out or transferring often beats just letting it rot

(1) The opportunity cost compounds daily

Server bills, domain costs, and mental real estate look small individually, but "I should get back to it someday" keeps blocking new ideas. Every sleeping project is another mental tab left open.

(2) Code depreciates over time

The stack ages, APIs change, and security patches pile up. "I'll sell it later, in six months" is almost always worse than selling it today. Right now is usually the least-outdated version it will ever be.

(3) There's a market of people who'd rather not build from scratch

People who have the idea but find implementation daunting, people who want to validate a niche that's already been proven, people who want to use a toy project as real operating experience. To them, your MVP isn't "a junk repo" — it's a time-saving package.

(4) Getting fixated on success stories leaves you with no exit

Indie hacker feeds are full of survivorship bias. In a reality where more than half of products sit at $0, the equation "if I can't grow it, it's worth zero" is often just wrong. Value is measured not by "my passion" but by a reason someone else would willingly spend their time on it.

(5) Handing it off is reallocation, not giving up

Transferring a project isn't abandoning a dream — it's closer to finding the dream's next owner. You get to focus on your next side project, your day job, or just rest, and the buyer gets to run with a foundation that's already in place.
